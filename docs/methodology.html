      Data, methods and how to read them

      Where these numbers come from, and how to use them well

      This site puts public schools, private schools, school districts and colleges in one state on a map, with their history. Every figure comes straight from files that the U.S. Department of Education and the Census Bureau publish. We standardise, organise and chart them; we do not estimate missing values or judge quality.

      DECISION-MAKERSUsing these numbers well OfficialsStaff

      These figures describe who is enrolled where, and how that has changed. They are a good starting point for a question and a poor place to stop. Here is how to get the most from them without overstating what they show.

      Good questions for this data

      • How has enrollment in a school, district or college changed over time?
      • What does the student population look like by race and ethnicity, grade or age?
      • Where are schools, colleges and private schools located, and what grades or programs do they offer?
      • How do tuition, aid and net price at a college compare with its own past?
      • How do a district’s revenue and spending per student break down?

      Questions it cannot answer

      • Which school or college is “best”. Nothing here is a quality measure.
      • Why a number changed. The data show what, not why.
      • How a school’s students perform on tests. Test scores are not included.
      • What any individual student or family experiences.
      • Anything newer than the latest release for each survey (see coverage).

      Terms that trip people up

      • Enrollment is a head count on one fall date, not a yearly average.
      • Students per teacher is not class size.
      • Free or reduced-price lunch is a rough income signal, and is unreliable where whole schools are enrolled automatically.
      • A share (%) says who is counted, not how any group is doing.
      • Years are school years: 2024 means 2024-25.

      Before you quote a number

      1. Say the year. “Fall 2024”, “school year 2023-24” or “the 2023-24 survey”. Private schools are surveyed every two years, so the latest survey may be older than the year you are looking at.
      2. Say the source. “According to the National Center for Education Statistics’ Common Core of Data” (public schools), “Private School Universe Survey” or “IPEDS” (colleges). Each chart in the chart guide below names its survey.
      3. Check the release. Newer data are marked preliminary or provisional and can be revised. The coverage table shows which stage each year is in.
      4. Look for blanks. An empty bar or “—” means the value was missing, hidden for privacy, or not collected. It does not mean zero.
      5. Compare like with like. Same survey, same definition, ideally the same school or college over time. Differences between places usually reflect size, location and mission before anything else.

      Wording that holds up

      Say

      • “Enrollment was 612 in fall 2023, down from 655 in fall 2019.”
      • “Hispanic or Latino students were 31% of students counted in 2024.”
      • “The college’s published in-state tuition and fees were $9,315 in 2023-24.”

      Avoid

      • “This school is better or worse than that one.”
      • “Enrollment fell because the school got worse.”
      • “Only 20% of students graduate”, from a chart that shows counts of graduates or one cohort’s rate.

      SOURCESWhere the data come from Everyone

      Three federal surveys supply the people and places. Two Census Bureau products supply the geography. All are public and free to use.

      Public schools and districts

      Common Core of Data (CCD)

      Each year, every state education agency reports its schools and districts to the National Center for Education Statistics (NCES).

      Contains
      School and district directories, enrollment by grade, race/ethnicity and sex, free and reduced-price lunch eligibility, teachers (FTE), program characteristics, and for districts: finance, graduates, dropouts, English learners and students with disabilities.

      How a number gets from the federal file to your screen

      1. DownloadThe original file from NCES or the Census Bureau, unchanged.
      2. CheckThe pipeline compares each file’s columns to what it expects and stops if anything is added, dropped or renamed.
      3. StandardiseCodes become labels; blanks, suppressed and “not applicable” stay distinct from zero.
      4. ValidateAutomated checks for duplicates, impossible values and mismatched totals must pass.
      5. PublishCompact data files are saved with this site. Your browser reads them directly; no server or account is involved.

      METHODSHow the numbers are handled AnalystsStaff

      A few rules apply everywhere on this site.

      Missing is never zero

      If a value was not reported, was hidden to protect privacy, or does not apply, the chart leaves a gap. A real zero is drawn as zero.

      Shares are not rates

      A bar showing that a group is 22% of graduates says who graduated. It does not say how likely that group is to graduate; that needs each group’s enrollment too.

      School years are labelled by their start

      2024 means the 2024-25 school year. Colleges use the fall of the file’s year. Private schools appear in survey years only.

      Nothing is ranked or scored

      There are no rankings, grades or stars. Where a state “typical” band appears, it is labelled context and shown only for years with at least 10 operating schools.

      Releases get revised

      NCES publishes data as preliminary, then provisional, then final; IPEDS replaces provisional files with revised ones. The coverage table shows each year’s stage.

      Totals are not always the sum of parts

      Detail rows can be hidden when counts are small, and subtotals are published alongside detail rows. We never add subtotals to details, so totals may exceed the visible parts.

      For analysts: value flags, identifiers and column rules
      • Flags. Source codes such as −1 (missing), −2 (not applicable), −3 (suppressed), M, N or text flags become empty values. A companion column (value_flag or <column>_flag) records why: reported, derived, adjusted, missing, not_applicable, not_reported, suppressed, not_meeting_standards.
      • IPEDS keeps negative codes as published, because in code columns they are labelled categories (for example “Not available”) and finance values can truly be negative. NCES’s own status columns appear as <column>_flag. Many IPEDS derived tables store numbers as text, with “.” or a letter for missing.
      • Identifiers are text with leading zeros preserved: ncessch (12-digit school), leaid (7-digit district), unitid (college), ppin (private school).
      • Derived values (students per teacher, lunch share, state median and middle half, year summary) are computed by the pipeline’s derive step from the published tables and are stored under data/derived/ and data/meta/. Students per teacher is left empty when teacher FTE is zero.
      • Dictionary. Every table column has an entry (label, description, unit, first and last year, caveat) built from NCES’s own documentation. Hover or focus a field name in a detail panel to read it.

      KNOWN LIMITSWhat to keep in mind, source by source Everyone

      Each source has quirks that can change how a number should be read. The short version comes first; open a group for the detail.

      Public schools (CCD)

      In short: the newest year may be incomplete; lunch eligibility is unreliable where schools enroll everyone automatically; small counts are hidden.

      • The latest year may be a preliminary directory with no enrollment, staff or lunch files yet. The map opens on the latest year that has enrollment.
      • Free and reduced-price lunch counts are not comparable across schools or years where the Community Eligibility Provision or similar rules apply, because eligibility may be reported for all students or carried forward.
      • Race and ethnicity categories were revised in 2009-10 (adding “Two or more races”). Totals are comparable; some rows are subtotals (“No Category Codes”, “Education Unit Total”) and are never added to detail rows.
      • Counts can be suppressed to protect privacy. District totals can differ slightly from the sum of school rows because of reconciliation and adult-education adjustments.
      • Status (open, closed, new, future, changed boundary) is as reported for that year. Closed schools are hidden unless you ask for them.
      • District finance amounts are in dollars of the year, not adjusted for inflation, and item definitions changed over time. The graduate and dropout files hold counts, not rates.
      • Before 2014-15 the files use older layouts: codes were converted to modern labels, and locale codes before 2006 use an older 8-category scheme. In 1997-98 to 2013-14 some states reported totals without race and sex detail, so detail rows may not add up. School years 1986-87 to 1996-97 exist only as fixed-width text and are not included.
      Locations and boundaries

      In short: most points are exact; some are approximate and drawn with a dotted outline.

      • Public school coordinates come from NCES EDGE (2014-15 onward). Where a year has no EDGE file yet, each school’s most recent EDGE point is used; schools with no earlier point, and colleges or private schools without source coordinates, were located from their address. PO boxes cannot be located and some addresses do not match.
      • IPEDS directory files carry coordinates only from about 2008, so institutions that closed earlier have no map location.
      • District boundaries are TIGER/Line vintages (as of January 1 of the labelled year). The newest school year reuses the latest vintage, so a recent boundary change may not appear yet.
      Private schools (PSS)

      In short: a survey every two years, and not every school answers.

      • The survey is conducted every two years (fall of the first year shown). The map shows the latest survey that began in or before the selected year, and says so.
      • School names and permanent IDs are in the public files from 2005-06 (and a few earlier years), so earlier surveys cannot be linked to schools or mapped. They remain in the data tables.
      • Questionnaire item numbers belong to each survey, and value labels follow the 2023-24 codebook, so definitions can differ in earlier years. NCES may impute values (see the imputation flags).
      Colleges (IPEDS)

      In short: different surveys use different definitions, so charts from different tabs are not interchangeable.

      • Fall enrollment race/ethnicity categories changed in 2010, so the race charts start there. 12-month headcount, completions (awards, not students) and admissions are separate surveys with their own definitions.
      • Student aid columns ending in a digit (for example npist0) are positional: the same name refers to different award years in different files.
      • Finance forms differ by institution type (public GASB, private nonprofit FASB, for-profit) and are only roughly comparable. The “every year” finance charts group the three forms into shared categories and show any remainder as “Other”.
      • Graduation-rate files hold cohort counts, exclusions and completions; a rate is computed only within one cohort. Faculty salaries for 2016-2020 are published only as total outlays, so those years are blank.
      • IPEDS files before 2000 use different layouts and many more table families. They are in the data tables, but not on the map or in the charts, and column definitions may differ.

      TECHNICAL REFERENCEReuse the data or rebuild it DevelopersAnalysts

      Everything the site displays is a plain file you can download. There is no API and no database server.

      Open files

      Data are Parquet files, one per table and year:

      data/<dataset>/<table>/<year>.parquet

      • ccd, pss, ipeds: the survey tables as published, standardised.
      • derived: school metrics and profiles computed from them.
      • geo: points, district boundaries and the search index.
      • meta: dictionary, value labels, row counts, release versions.

      Guarantees

      • Files are deterministic: the same input always writes the same bytes.
      • A new NCES release that adds, drops or renames a column stops the build instead of being guessed at.
      • Validation (uniqueness, ranges, totals, file counts) must pass before any data are committed.
      • Raw downloads are never committed; every step can be re-run from the original sources.

      Try it now

      Any tool that reads Parquet over the web can query these files. For example, in DuckDB:

      SELECT *
FROM read_parquet('…/data/ccd/membership/2024.parquet')
LIMIT 10;

      Or in Python with pandas:

      import pandas as pd
df = pd.read_parquet('…/data/ipeds/efadist/2024.parquet')
df.head()
      For developers: rebuild, extend, contribute
      • Pipeline. Python and DuckDB, in pipeline/. Commands include update (download, check, standardise, derive), validate and derive. Column maps are year-ranged YAML files, so a new release is usually a short edit.
      • One state at a time. The state shown is set by a single configuration file; another state is built by adding a file and running the pipeline.
      • Web app. Svelte, MapLibre, DuckDB-WASM (reads the Parquet files in your browser) and Observable Plot, in web/. It is static, with no backend, and runs from any web host.
      • Tests. pytest for the pipeline, vitest for chart logic, and Playwright checks for the map, charts, tooltips, touch layout and accessibility (axe, light and dark).
      • Accessibility. Charts have text alternatives, tooltips are available on touch and keyboard, and colour is never the only cue (markers also differ in shape).
